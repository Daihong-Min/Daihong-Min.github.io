\sechead{Job Market Paper}
\textbf{Financial Sanctions and Dollar Dominance in Trade Invoicing}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\small
\textbf{Abstract.} Does the use of financial sanctions weaken dollar dominance in trade invoicing? Using a panel of FX-neutral import invoicing shares for 88 countries over 2012--2023, I examine how responses to U.S. sanctions against Chinese entities vary with baseline import exposure to China. For a one-standard-deviation increase in log sanctions intensity, a 10 percentage points (pp) higher China import share is associated with a contemporaneous Chinese renminbi (RMB) response that is 0.19 pp higher and a U.S. dollar (USD) response that is 0.84 pp lower. The RMB exposure gradient is flatter among destinations whose imports from China have low working-capital intensity. I develop a model in which exporters balance the dollar's normal-time financing and hedging benefits against sanctions-related disruptions to usable credit. The model identifies conditions under which greater working-capital dependence lowers the threshold for switching to RMB invoicing. In the reference quantitative specification, removing working-capital heterogeneity prevents the model from reproducing the low-WCI attenuation, even after re-fitting the remaining parameters. A separate retrospective comparison finds modest improvements in subsequent invoicing paths when working-capital heterogeneity is included. These findings point to the reliability of working-capital finance as a channel through which sanctions can reshape currency choice along trade links to China.\par
}

\sechead{Working Paper Abstracts}
\textbf{Promotional Slack and the Strategic Pass-Through of Local Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Taxes}\par
{\small (with Yijoong Won and \href{https://kiesel.ucdavis.edu/}{Kristin Kiesel})\par}
\vspace{3pt}
{\small
\textbf{Abstract.} The effective price a consumer pays at store combines a regular price with temporary promotions. A tax can raise the regular price, cut the frequency of discounts, or both. The effective price hides which margin moves. The paper asks how retailers split a permanent tax between the two. A simple model makes the regular price costly to change and promotions cheaper but bounded by pre-tax slack, so low-slack markets raise the regular price and high-slack markets cut promotions. We study the 2018 San Francisco sugar-sweetened beverage tax with NielsenIQ scanner data, in a difference-in-differences with Sacramento as the control and a local-projections version for the dynamics. On average the tax passes through to consumers at about 73 percent, in every channel but one. Low-slack grocery stores and soft drinks move the regular price; high-slack discount stores cut promotions and hold the regular price nearly flat. Because incidence can run through promotions that the effective price miss, the distributional reading of these taxes depends on which margin moves.\par
}

\vspace{2.5em}

\textbf{Does Habitual Demand Survive a Household Health Shock? Evidence from Retail Diabetes-Device Purchases}{\small (with Yifan Xie)\par}
\vspace{3pt}
{\small
\textbf{Abstract.} Soft-drink purchases are highly persistent, and macroeconomic models use habit formation in individual goods to explain sluggish demand and incomplete price pass-through. Evidence for this mechanism largely comes from responses to price, tax, and income shocks, which may also reflect slow price adjustment or delayed awareness. We examine whether this persistence extends to a household-specific health event. Using Nielsen scanner data linked to survey-reported diagnoses, we identify retail monitoring onset from households’ first observed purchases of glucose meters or test strips. Local projection difference-in-differences estimates compare these households with those recording neither a monitoring purchase nor a diabetes diagnosis. Regular soda purchases decline within two quarters and remain lower for two years. The average reduction is 70.5 ounces per household per quarter, approximately 11 percent of purchases before onset. The decline in regular soda’s share is accompanied by a corresponding increase in diet soda’s share. Among households whose monitoring purchases precede the reported diagnosis year, the decline begins in the purchase quarter. The adjustment is concentrated in the first two quarters, while roughly 90 percent of baseline purchases remain. These findings suggest that households respond promptly to the onset of retail monitoring but make limited changes to an otherwise persistent purchasing pattern.\par
}
