\sechead{Job Market Paper}
\textbf{Financial Sanctions and Dollar Dominance in Trade Invoicing} \href{https://www.daihongmin.com/jmp.pdf}{\textcolor{blue}{[draft]}}\par
\vspace{0.5pt}
{\small
\textbf{Abstract.} This paper documents signs that the long-standing dominance of the U.S. dollar in global trade invoicing is beginning to falter, a development associated with U.S. financial sanctions. Theoretically, I propose a novel mechanism linking invoicing currency choice to sanctions: invoicing in dollars exposes firms to uncertainty about future cash flows under sanctions, which can lead creditors to limit working-capital lending. Firms balance this financing risk against the benefits of dollar invoicing in terms of liquidity and reduced exchange-rate risk. Using country-level import invoicing data, I find that stronger U.S. sanctions against Chinese entities are associated with small but measurable additional declines in dollar invoicing shares and additional increases in renminbi invoicing shares in countries with greater pre-existing import exposure to China. For a 10-percentage-point higher initial share of imports from China, a one-standard-deviation increase in sanctions is associated with a 0.8-percentage-point larger decline in the dollar invoicing share and a 0.2-percentage-point larger rise in the renminbi invoicing share on impact. Consistent with the mechanism, suggestive evidence indicates that the renminbi response is weaker where imports from China are concentrated in industries with lower working-capital requirement. These findings suggest that sanctions can weaken dollar dominance by making dollar invoicing a source of financing risk.\par
}

\sechead{Working Paper Abstracts}
\textbf{Pre-Tax Promotional Slack and the Strategic Pass-Through of Local Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Taxes}
{\small (with Yijoong Won and \href{https://kiesel.ucdavis.edu/}{Kristin Kiesel})\par}
{\small
\textbf{Abstract.} A retailer can pass through an excise tax to consumers in two ways: by raising the list or regular price, or by quietly reducing promotions previously offered. We study which of these strategies retailers choose after the 2018 San Francisco sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) tax. We develop a simple model positing that changing the regular price is relatively costly to retailers, while cutting promotions is relatively cheaper but limited by each market’s pre-tax promotional slack, meaning the discounts it already offers that it could withdraw. The model predicts that markets with ample slack pass the tax through by cutting promotions, while markets with little slack raise the regular price. Using NielsenIQ store-week scanner data and local projection difference-in-differences estimates, and the Sacramento market as a control, we find that consumers pay about 73 percent of the one-cent-per-ounce tax, that promotion frequency fell by about 2 percentage points, and that taxed volume decreased by 9 percent. Studies that measure pass-through using regular or list prices alone will not capture price changes consumers actually face and average pass-throughs previously reported in the literature hide the mechanisms that resulted in these changes. Few studies published to date provide a disaggregated analysis by type of retailer. We find that grocery stores raise regular prices for SSBs by about 8 percent. In contrast, discount stores, which have more pre-tax promotional slack, raise regular prices of SSBs by less than 5 percent but cut promotions sharply, and as a result, the largest effective-price increase we observe in our data (about 11 percent). Prices for taxed sparkling water change very little across all retailers but lose 11 percent in sales volume.\par
}

\textbf{Fast but Modest: Cognitive Transition and Soda Purchases among Households Managing Diabetes} {\small (with Yifan Xie)\par}
{\small
\textbf{Abstract.} Dietary change is central to managing diabetes, but the timing of shifts in household purchasing behavior is difficult to observe and may occur prior to a reported diagnosis. We study how soda purchases change when a household takes a step toward managing diabetes. We call this step a cognitive transition and date it by the household's first observed retail purchase of a glucose meter or test strips, separately from the diagnosis year the household reports in a survey. Using NielsenIQ household scanner data linked to NielsenIQ PanelView surveys and a local projection difference-in-differences design, we compare these households with households that record neither a monitoring purchase nor a diabetes diagnosis. Regular soda purchases are already lower in the transition quarter and stay lower for two years. At the household level, the average decline is 70.5 fluid ounces per quarter, about 11 percent of purchases before the transition and about three quarters of the average response to a beverage tax. Among households whose monitoring purchase precedes the reported diagnosis year, the decline begins before that year. Diet soda replaces less than half of the lost volume. The decline does not grow after the first quarter, so a modest response need not reflect slow adjustment.\par
}
